# Technical // From Prompts to Prototypes

I went into ProtoSem expecting the technical track to start with a tool. Instead, it started with a warning: *don't use technology just because it's available.* That one line, delivered almost casually in a Week 0 talk on prompt engineering, ended up shaping how I approached every technical concept that came after it — from AI tools to algorithms to my first real app build.

## Lesson One: A Tool Is Only as Useful as the Problem It's Matched To

The Week 0 Tech Talk on prompt engineering wasn't really about clever prompt tricks. It was about *intentionality* — picking the right AI tool for a specific need, inside a specific domain, instead of throwing the newest tool at every problem. That idea sounds obvious written down, but it's surprisingly easy to violate in practice. It's tempting to reach for the most powerful tool available. ProtoSem's framing flipped that instinct: reach for the *right* tool, and only after you actually understand the problem.

That framing kept resurfacing. Week 1's Tech Talks — recommendation algorithms, Power BI, an emerging tool called Base44 — weren't presented as "here's what's trending." Each one came with a question attached: *what specific kind of decision does this actually help someone make?* Recommendation algorithms shape what people see without them realizing it. Power BI turns scattered data into something a business can actually act on. Neither is impressive on its own — they're only useful once paired with an actual decision that needs making.

## Lesson Two: Before Code, There's Logic

Week 2 is where "intentional technology" stopped being a philosophy and became a practical skill. Before we touched an actual app-building platform, we spent a full day on algorithms and flowcharts — and not through lecture. We were handed situations and videos and asked to figure out the underlying sequence of steps ourselves, then translate that into a flowchart.

That exercise reframed what "programming" even means to me. It's not about syntax first — it's about being able to break something messy and human (a task, a process, a decision) into a sequence clear enough that a machine — or another person — could follow it without guessing. Flowcharts made that logic visible before a single line of code existed. If the logic didn't hold up on paper, it wasn't going to hold up in an app either.

## Lesson Three: Play First, Precision Later

Scratch came right after the algorithms session, and the contrast was almost funny. After a day of structured flowcharts, we were suddenly building memes and silly interactive projects in a drag-and-block interface. But underneath the humor, the same skill was being exercised: translating an idea in your head into a sequence of instructions a computer can actually execute.

What I appreciated about starting with Scratch instead of a "real" programming language is that it removed the fear of syntax errors entirely. Nobody was worried about a missing semicolon — we were worried about whether the *logic* of our little project made sense. That's a smart sequencing choice: let people fail at logic in a low-stakes, funny environment before asking them to also fight a compiler.

## Lesson Four: Real Constraints Change Everything

The real technical shift happened on Day 4 of Week 2, with MIT App Inventor. This wasn't an exercise anymore — it was tied to an actual, validated problem statement we'd chosen as a team. Suddenly the algorithms and flowchart thinking from two days earlier had a real job to do: mapping out how a real app should behave, not just how a hypothetical process should flow.

Working with block-based programming in App Inventor kept the emphasis where it belonged — on structure and logic, not on wrestling with a new language's quirks. But because the problem behind it was real (and had already been through a validation step), the stakes felt different. It wasn't a demo anymore. It was the first rough shape of something we actually wanted to work.

## The Technical Thread So Far

Looking back, the technical arc of these three weeks hasn't been about accumulating tools. It's been: *intentional tool selection → visible logic → low-stakes experimentation → real application.* Each week added one more layer of rigor onto the last, but the throughline never changed — understand the problem and the logic before reaching for the tool.

If Week 0 taught me to ask "why this tool," and Week 2 taught me to ask "what's the logic before I build," I'm curious what technical question Week 3 is going to hand me next.
