# Innovation // Prototyping Under Constraint

If there's one myth ProtoSem has quietly dismantled for me, it's that innovation starts with a good idea. Three weeks in, I'd argue it actually starts with a good *constraint* — a limited set of materials, a limited amount of time, a limited (and validated) problem to solve. Every real building exercise so far has forced us to work inside a box, and every time, the box turned out to be the point.

## Constraint #1: A Handful of Spaghetti Sticks

The earliest version of this lesson showed up in Week 0's Marshmallow Tower Challenge — spaghetti, tape, thread, and one marshmallow, structured into a race against the clock. The instinct in that room was to plan the "perfect" tower first and build it once. That instinct failed almost immediately.

What actually worked was the opposite: build something rough, watch it fall, notice *why*, adjust, rebuild. It's the entire innovation cycle — ideate, prototype, test, iterate — compressed into twenty minutes with snack food. The lesson wasn't really about towers. It was that constraints (limited materials, limited time) don't kill creativity; they force it to move faster and get honest with itself sooner.

## Constraint #2: A User You Don't Actually Know Yet

Week 1 introduced a subtler constraint — not a resource limit, but a *knowledge* limit. In the Design Thinking session applied to an eyewear retail scenario, the challenge wasn't coming up with ideas. Ideas are easy. The hard part was resisting the urge to design for an imagined customer instead of sitting with what an actual customer might need.

That same discipline showed up again in the Value Chain and SWOT Analysis exercise — instead of pitching a solution outright, we were forced to first map out where value was actually created (and where it was leaking) in a business. It's a constraint on enthusiasm, basically: you're not allowed to jump to "here's my idea" until you've mapped the terrain the idea has to survive in.

Prospect Theory added one more layer to this later in the week — a reminder that even a perfectly logical solution can fail if it ignores how people actually weigh gains and losses. That's a constraint too: your solution isn't just competing with the "right" answer, it's competing with human psychology.

## Constraint #3: A Problem That Has to Earn Its Place

Week 2 is where "constraint" stopped being a metaphor and became literal project scope. We spent nearly half a day just choosing a problem statement — and I underestimated how hard that would be. It's tempting to pick the first inconvenience that comes to mind. What the session with Arun pushed us toward was slower and more deliberate: is this problem actually relevant? Does it have real impact? Can we, realistically, do something about it?

The 5S approach at Forge added a practical layer to that same instinct — observing an environment carefully enough to notice what's actually inefficient, rather than assuming based on a five-minute glance. Both exercises were really about the same constraint: not every "problem" deserves your time, and figuring out which ones do is its own skill.

By Day 4, that discipline paid off directly. Before a single screen was designed in MIT App Inventor, we revisited and validated our chosen problem — checking it was still worth solving before committing real effort to building it. That validation step is easy to skip when you're excited to start building. Skipping it is also, I'd guess, how most half-finished, misdirected projects get started.

## What Constraint Actually Buys You

Across all three weeks, the pattern repeats: fewer resources, fewer assumptions, or fewer allowed problem statements didn't slow innovation down — it sharpened it. The spaghetti tower forced fast iteration. The design thinking sessions forced real user empathy instead of assumption. The problem validation process forced us to earn the right to start building.

None of this matches the popular image of innovation as a lightning-bolt idea. It's slower, more deliberate, and honestly a little uncomfortable — you spend more time questioning your own idea than defending it. But by the time we opened MIT App Inventor with a validated problem in hand, I understood why that discomfort is the actual work. The tower doesn't fall because you lacked creativity. It falls because you skipped the constraint that would have made you test your assumptions sooner.
