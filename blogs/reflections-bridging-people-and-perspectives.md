# Reflections // Bridging People & Perspectives

There's a version of "teamwork" that gets taught in slides — communication, trust, shared goals. And then there's the version you actually live through, which looks a lot less polished: a Rock-Paper-Scissors icebreaker with strangers, a spaghetti tower that collapses twice, a Scratch project that turns into an inside joke. Three weeks into ProtoSem, I've started noticing that the "soft" parts of this program were never really soft. They were the foundation everything technical eventually stood on.

## Strangers, By Design

Week 0 didn't ease us into anything. It threw us straight into a room of people we'd never met and asked us to talk, play, and observe together before we'd even learned each other's names properly. The Rock-Paper-Scissors activity was silly on the surface, but it did something specific: it made the first conversation happen *before* the awkwardness had time to set in.

What followed was more introspective — 16 Personalities, Zen Pencils comics, sitting with how differently two people can read the exact same story. I remember picking a comic and explaining why it resonated with me, then watching a teammate pick the *same* comic for a completely different reason. That wasn't a coincidence, and it wasn't really about comics. It was the first hint that "the problem" or "the solution" I see clearly might look nothing like what the person next to me sees — not because one of us is wrong, but because we're built to notice different things.

Then came the Marshmallow Tower Challenge, which is where the reflection stopped being theoretical. Our first tower fell. Not because we didn't care, but because we hadn't yet learned to actually listen to each other under pressure. The second attempt worked because something had shifted — less talking over each other, more building on what the last person said. That's a small, physical proof of something I'd otherwise just call "trust."

## The In-Between Moments Matter More Than You Think

By Week 1, the cohort didn't feel like strangers anymore, but the bonding didn't stop — it just moved into different spaces. Onam wasn't on the technical syllabus, but it did something the workshops couldn't: it let people be people, outside of problem statements and frameworks. I noticed conversations during Onam went deeper than most of our structured team discussions, simply because nobody was trying to solve anything.

It's easy to treat a "break" day as a pause in the real work. I don't think it was a pause. I think it was where a lot of the trust we'd built in Week 0 actually got tested and reinforced — quietly, without anyone announcing it.

## Collaboration Gets Harder (and More Honest) Once There's Something to Build

Week 2 is where I really saw the difference our first two weeks had made. When we sat down with Scratch, the goal was almost absurdly low-stakes — build something funny, experiment, make memes. But watching how naturally the team split up ideas, tried each other's suggestions without ego, and laughed through the failed attempts told me something: the trust from Week 0's marshmallow towers hadn't gone anywhere. It had just been waiting for a real task to show up in.

That same dynamic carried into problem selection and MIT App Inventor. Choosing a problem statement as a team is a strange kind of vulnerable — everyone has an opinion about what's "worth solving," and not all of those opinions survive the discussion. What made that process work wasn't a framework. It was the same thing that made the marshmallow tower eventually stand: people who'd learned, over two weeks, how to disagree without dismissing each other.

## What I'm Taking From This

If I'm honest, none of the technical content so far — not the algorithms, not the app-building — required a huge leap for me. What actually shifted was subtler: I've started noticing perspective as a variable, not a given. Before assuming a problem is "obvious," I catch myself asking who else is looking at this, and what they might be seeing that I'm not.

ProtoSem hasn't just been teaching us how to build something. It's been quietly teaching us how to build *with* people — which, three weeks in, feels like the harder and more useful skill of the two.
